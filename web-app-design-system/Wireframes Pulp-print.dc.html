01Display name, not real name — with an 11–16 audience the safer default is a handle, and the placeholder should suggest one.
04Availability implies display names are unique. That is a database constraint plus a debounced lookup — check on the server, not just in the client, or two people racing will both be told “free”.
05Red #C41031 is the fifth accent, and its only job is “problem”. It lives in pulp.css as --color-problem — darker than the monogram red, because a fill carrying 14px bold text needs 4.5:1 and the brighter red only reached 4.0:1. Never use it decoratively.
06Suggestions matter more than the error. A taken name with no way forward is where a 12-year-old gives up; three tappable alternatives keep them moving.
07Banned words (profanity and slurs) get their own state, deliberately vague: “that name isn't allowed here”, never repeating the word back or naming what tripped it. Explaining the filter teaches people how to beat it.
08The word list must live on the SERVER, from a maintained library plus your own additions — never in the browser bundle, where it is both bypassable and a public file of slurs. Normalise first (l33tspeak, repeated letters, stripped punctuation) or “d4mn_” walks straight through.
09The same filter belongs on review text (D2), custom list titles (C2) and missing-book requests (D3) — every field a stranger can read. A filter on names alone is security theatre.
02No photo upload in v1 — a coloured monogram instead. That removes file validation, cropping, storage and image moderation from the build, which is a lot of work avoided for a 78px square.
03Seven flat spot colours, not a tinted ramp — four of them are the theme's own accents (orange, lime, blue, pink) so a monogram never looks foreign next to the UI. The light three take ink letters, the dark four take cream.

THINGS TO DECIDE BEFORE ANYONE WRITES CODE
  1. Server-rendered book pages. A3 is your search-traffic page. If the title, summary and reviews arrive by script after load, search engines often see an empty page. Decide the rendering approach before you pick a framework, not after.
  2. Survey answers are your data model. The genre list in B4, the filters in A2 and the tags on A3 must be one shared list in code. If they drift apart, tag-matching recommendations quietly stop working.
  3. Moderation. D2 posts public text written by 11–16 year olds. Even a manual “report” link and an owner inbox is enough for v1 — but choose deliberately.
  4. Cold start. If the survey matches nothing, B6 has no recommendations. Plan the fallback now: most-reviewed books in the chosen genres.
  5. Cache the API. Google Books and Open Library rate-limit. Store the books people actually look at rather than calling the API on every page view.
